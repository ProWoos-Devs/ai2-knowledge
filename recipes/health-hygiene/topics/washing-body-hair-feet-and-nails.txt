Washing your body, hair, feet and nails

Written for AI-2. Directed to children, parents and teachers.

EVERYDAY CARE

Personal hygiene means habits for keeping your body clean. It includes washing skin and hair, caring for feet and nails, and keeping hands clean. These habits help remove dirt and reduce some infection risks. Washing is one part of staying healthy; it does not make a person immune to every illness. The sources below describe routine care, not treatments for a rash or an infection. [HYG, Personal hygiene; HAIR, Key points; FOOT, Basic care for healthy feet; web.]

For the vulva, the outside genital area, rinse with water only; do not wash inside the vagina, an internal passage. That specific care differs from washing ordinary skin with soap. [PERIOD, Other hygiene practices; web.]

HAIR AND SCALP

The scalp is the skin on your head. It makes a natural oil that helps protect the skin. Oil, sweat, dirt and dead skin can build up. Wash your hair regularly, using soap or a shampoo suited to your hair, and clean water. Different hair types need different care; the source does not set one washing timetable for everyone. Do not share combs, brushes or towels. Some infections can spread through shared personal items. [HAIR, Why it's important, Ringworm and Steps to take; web.]

FEET AND SOCKS

Wash your feet each day and dry them completely, including between your toes. Keep toenails short and clean. If you wear socks, change them at least daily. Clean, dry feet matter because fungi, which are one kind of microbe, can grow well in warm, damp places. Footwear should fit well and protect your feet. In places where soil may contain human feces and hookworm, a parasitic worm, is common, walking barefoot can allow infection. [FOOT, Key points, Ringworm and fungal nail infections, Hookworm and Basic care for healthy feet; web.]

HANDS AND NAILS

Keep fingernails short and clean, and wash underneath them with soap and water when washing your hands. Clean nail clippers before use. Do not bite nails, bite off loose pieces of skin beside them, or cut the cuticle, the small strip of skin at the nail's base. The cuticle helps protect against infection. Dirt and germs can collect under long nails even if the top of the hand looks clean. [NAIL, Overview and Everyday tips; web.]

NOTICE A PROBLEM

Pain or swelling around a nail, or a nail that becomes unusually thick, may mean an infection that needs medical attention. Adults can help children notice cuts, sores or swelling on their feet. Routine washing is not a way to identify which germ caused a problem or to choose a medicine. [NAIL, Overview; FOOT, Basic care for healthy feet; web.]

WHEN WATER OR SUPPLIES ARE SHORT

These sources assume washing water and basic supplies are available. They do not give a complete body-washing method without water. The supported fallback for hands is narrower: with water but no soap, rubbing hands under the water removes some germs, less effectively than soap. That does not establish a replacement for every other washing task. [HANDFAQ, What if I have water but no soap to wash my hands?; web.]
