Moving and playing every day

Written for AI-2. Directed to children, parents and teachers.

MOVEMENT COUNTS

Physical activity means movement that uses your body's energy. Children aged 6 to 17 are recommended by CDC to get at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity each day. The ages in this Knowledge Pack, 6 to 12, fall inside that group. Active games, brisk walking and dancing can count; activity does not have to mean joining a sports team. [PLAY, Children and adolescents 6 to 17; PLAYTYPES, What counts as physical activity for children and adolescents and Examples; web.]

MODERATE AND VIGOROUS

Moderate activity makes your heart beat faster and your breathing work harder than when resting. A useful general guide is that you can talk but not sing during it. Vigorous activity is harder: you can usually say only a few words before taking a breath. Running games and lively dancing can be vigorous. The same kind of activity can feel different depending on how hard a person does it. [PLAY, How do I measure aerobic intensity?; PLAYTYPES, What counts as physical activity for children and adolescents; web.]

The daily time can include different activities. For example, 20 minutes of brisk walking, 20 minutes of an active game and 20 minutes of lively dancing add up to 60 minutes. Those activities need to be active enough; an hour sitting beside a game is not an hour of moderate activity. The example shows addition, not a required daily timetable. [ACTIVITY p. 48; PLAYTYPES, What counts as physical activity for children and adolescents; web.]

MUSCLES AND BONES

Most of the daily activity should be aerobic, meaning activity that makes the heart and breathing work harder. Include vigorous activity on at least 3 days each week. Include muscle-strengthening activity on at least 3 days, and bone-strengthening activity on at least 3 days; they need not all happen on the same days. Climbing or body-weight games can work muscles. Running, hopping and jumping can strengthen bones. One game can fit more than one group: running and jumping during a game are not separate time targets to add on top of the hour. [PLAY, Children and adolescents 6 to 17; PLAYTYPES, opening key points and Examples; web.]

CHOOSE PLAY THAT FITS

Choose enjoyable activities suited to your age and skills. Children do not usually need a formal weightlifting program. Families can be active together through ordinary games, walking or dancing. A teacher can make room in the day for activity instead of expecting sitting and screens to fill every break. [PLAY, What are age-appropriate activities?; PLAYTYPES, What counts as physical activity for children and adolescents; EAT, How can I help my child be more active?; web.]

Start with activities suited to your abilities and increase activity gradually if you are not used to it. Children with disabilities should work with a health or activity professional when available to choose suitable activity. If the full recommendations are not possible, be as active as your abilities allow. The hour is not a pass-or-fail test. This Knowledge Pack cannot choose an individual exercise plan for you. [ACTIVITY pp. 52–53.]
