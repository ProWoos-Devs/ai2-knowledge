Getting enough sleep and rest

Search terms: sleep, bedtime, tired, rest, how much sleep, nine to twelve hours, wake up, sleep routine, trouble sleeping, sleepy at school, screens before bed.

Written for AI-2. Directed to children, parents and teachers.

HOW MUCH SLEEP?

Children aged 6 to 12 are generally recommended to get 9 to 12 hours of sleep in each 24-hour day. This is a range, not a rule that every child needs exactly the same amount. The recommendation is about time asleep, not simply time spent lying in bed. Younger children and teenagers have different age ranges, so the adult recommendation is not the right one for a primary-school child. [SLEEP, Recommended hours of sleep; SLEEPH, Getting enough sleep; web.]

For example, being asleep from 20:30 until 07:00 gives 10 hours and 30 minutes. It is within the range for a child aged 6 to 12. If a child spends part of that time awake, actual sleep is shorter. The example is arithmetic, not a bedtime every household must follow. A helping adult can use the family's waking time to plan enough opportunity for sleep. [SLEEP, Recommended hours of sleep; SLEEPH, What to do; web.]

WHY SLEEP MATTERS

Enough good-quality sleep helps attention and memory and can improve mood. Feeling tired does not mean a child is lazy. A person may have had too little sleep, interrupted sleep or a sleep problem. Quality matters as well as the number of hours: sleep should be refreshing rather than leaving you very sleepy despite apparently spending enough time asleep. [SLEEPH, Health benefits of sleep, Sleep quality and Sleep disorders; web.]

HABITS THAT CAN HELP

Try to go to bed and get up at consistent times each day. Make the sleeping space as quiet, comfortable and cool as circumstances allow. Turn electronic devices off at least 30 minutes before bedtime. Regular activity during the day can help sleep too. Avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening; caffeine is a stimulating substance found in some drinks. These habits can help, but do not promise an instant cure for every sleep problem. [SLEEPH, What to do and Sleep disorders; web.]

For adults: repeated waking, difficulty falling asleep, or continued sleepiness after enough time asleep are reasons to seek health advice when available. A child's own report matters: saying “I keep waking up” gives more information than assuming the child slept because the room was quiet. The source recommends discussing regular sleep problems with a health professional; this topic cannot identify their cause. [SLEEPH, Sleep quality and Management; SLEEP, closing paragraph; web.]

WHEN THE HOUSEHOLD CANNOT KEEP A ROUTINE

A shared room, noise or disrupted daily life can make these habits difficult. The recommendations describe helpful conditions, not a judgment on a family that cannot provide them. The sources give no medicine dose or method of replacing sleep with another activity. Resting quietly while awake is not counted as hours asleep in the example above. [SLEEPH, What to do and Sleep quality; SLEEP, Recommended hours of sleep; web.]
