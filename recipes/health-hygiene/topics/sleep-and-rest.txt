Getting enough sleep and rest

Search terms: sleep, bedtime, tired, rest, how much sleep, nine to twelve hours, wake up, sleep routine, trouble sleeping, sleepy at school, screens before bed.

Written for AI-2. Directed to children, parents and teachers.

HOW MUCH SLEEP?

Children aged 6 to 12 are generally recommended to get 9 to 12 hours of sleep in each 24-hour day. This is a range: children do not all need exactly the same amount. Different ages need different amounts. CDC gives 10 to 13 hours, including naps, for ages 3 to 5, and 8 to 10 hours for ages 13 to 17. Use the range for your age. [SLEEP, Recommended hours of sleep; SLEEPH, Getting enough sleep; web.]

For example, sleeping from 20:30 until 07:00 gives 10 hours and 30 minutes, within the range for ages 6 to 12. That is an example, not a bedtime every household must follow. A helping adult can use the family's waking time to plan enough opportunity for sleep. [SLEEP, Recommended hours of sleep; SLEEPH, What to do; web.]

WHY SLEEP MATTERS

Enough good-quality sleep helps attention and memory and can improve mood. Feeling tired does not mean a child is lazy. A person may have had too little sleep, interrupted sleep or a sleep problem. Quality matters as well as the number of hours: sleep should be refreshing rather than leaving you very sleepy despite apparently spending enough time asleep. [SLEEPH, Health benefits of sleep, Sleep quality and Sleep disorders; web.]

HABITS THAT CAN HELP

Try to go to bed and get up at consistent times each day. Make the sleeping space as quiet, comfortable and cool as circumstances allow. Turn electronic devices off at least 30 minutes before bedtime. Regular activity during the day can help sleep too. Avoid caffeine in the afternoon and evening; caffeine is a stimulating substance found in some drinks. These habits can help, but do not promise an instant cure for every sleep problem. [SLEEPH, What to do and Sleep disorders; web.]

For adults: repeated waking, difficulty falling asleep, or continued sleepiness after enough sleep are reasons to seek health advice when available. Listen when a child says “I keep waking up.” Regular sleep problems need discussion with a health professional; the cause is not always clear from how tired someone feels. [SLEEPH, Sleep quality and Management; SLEEP, closing paragraph; web.]

WHEN THE HOUSEHOLD CANNOT KEEP A ROUTINE

A shared room, noise or disrupted daily life can make these habits difficult. A family may not be able to provide a quiet room or a regular bedtime. That does not make a child's tiredness unimportant. This Knowledge Pack gives no sleep-medicine doses. [SLEEPH, What to do and Sleep quality; web.]
