WHAT MATTER IS

Matter is the scientific word for all physical stuff in the universe that has mass and takes up physical space. Everything you can touch, see, smell, or taste is made of matter, composed of countless tiny building blocks called atoms and molecules. On Earth, matter exists in three common states: solid, liquid, and gas.

SOLIDS

In a solid, the particles are packed tightly together and held in fixed positions. They cannot move freely; they can only vibrate gently back and forth in place. In some solids, such as salt crystals, the particles form a neat repeating pattern. In others, such as glass, they have no regular pattern.
- Properties: Solids have a definite, fixed shape and a definite volume.
- Examples: Rocks, wooden tables, metal spoons, and ice cubes. A solid keeps its shape whether you put it in a bowl, on a table, or in a backpack.

LIQUIDS

In a liquid, the particles are close together but are not held in fixed positions. They can tumble, slide, and flow over and around one another smoothly.
- Properties: Liquids have a definite volume, but no fixed shape. A liquid takes the exact shape of whatever container you pour it into.
- Examples: Water, milk, orange juice, and cooking oil. If you spill a glass of water, it spreads out across the table until stopped by a barrier.

GASES

In a gas, the particles have high energy and are spaced very far apart from each other. They zoom around in all directions at high speeds, bouncing off each other and off the walls of their container.
- Properties: Gases have no fixed shape and no fixed volume. A gas expands completely to fill any container it is put into.
- Examples: The air we breathe, helium inside party balloons, water vapor from a boiling kettle, and carbon dioxide bubbles in fizzy soda.

CHANGING STATES WITH HEAT

Matter changes from one state to another when you add or take away heat energy:
- Melting: Adding heat turns a solid into a liquid (ice melting into water at 0 °C).
- Freezing (solidifying): Cooling removes heat, turning a liquid into a solid (water freezing into ice).
- Evaporation and boiling: Adding heat turns a liquid into a gas (water boiling into invisible water vapor at 100 °C at sea level; a puddle drying in the sun is evaporation at a lower temperature).
- Condensation: Cooling removes heat from a gas, turning it back into a liquid (water vapor meeting a cold bathroom mirror and turning into water droplets).
The white cloud you see above a boiling kettle is not the gas itself. It is tiny droplets of liquid water that form when the invisible vapor cools in the air.
Through all these changes, the particles themselves stay the same; only their energy and arrangement change.
