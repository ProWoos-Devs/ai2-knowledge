Heat, temperature and conduction

Written for AI-2. Directed to children, parents and teachers.

HEAT VERSUS TEMPERATURE

Heat and temperature are closely related, but they are not the same thing:
- Thermal energy (heat) is the total amount of vibrating kinetic energy stored in the moving particles of an object. The faster the particles jiggle, the more heat energy they possess.
- Temperature is a measure of how hot or cold something is, telling us the average speed of those vibrating particles. Temperature is measured in degrees Celsius (°C) using a thermometer.
A giant swimming pool filled with warm 25 °C water contains much more total heat energy than a tiny cup of boiling tea at 100 °C, because the swimming pool has vastly more warm particles.

HEAT ALWAYS FLOWS FROM HOT TO COLD

Heat energy naturally flows from a warmer object to a cooler object until both reach the exact same temperature. When you hold an ice cube in your warm palm, your hand does not "absorb coldness"; instead, heat flows out of your warm hand into the cold ice, warming the ice until it melts and leaving your palm chilly.

THE THREE WAYS HEAT TRAVELS

1. Conduction: Heat traveling through a material, or between materials that touch. It works best in solids. Fast, jiggling particles bump into their cooler neighbors, passing the vibration down the line. Leaving a metal spoon in a pot of hot soup makes the handle hot through conduction.
2. Convection: Heat traveling through liquids and gases by circulating currents. Warm liquid or air expands, becomes less dense, and rises upward, while cooler fluid sinks down to replace it. This is why warm air rises to the ceiling of a heated room.
3. Radiation: Heat traveling as light waves, both the light we see and invisible infrared waves. These waves can cross empty space. The Sun's energy travels 150 million kilometers through the vacuum of space and warms the Earth through radiation.

THERMAL CONDUCTORS AND INSULATORS

- Thermal conductors: Materials that let heat flow through them quickly and easily. Metals like copper, aluminum, and iron are excellent heat conductors, which is why cooking frying pans are made of metal.
- Thermal insulators: Materials that block or slow down the transfer of heat. Wood, plastic, rubber, wool, feathers, and trapped air are superb insulators. This is why pan handles are wrapped in heat-resistant plastic or wood and winter coats are filled with fluffy down. A vacuum flask keeps drinks hot for hours because the gap between its two walls has almost no air, so there are very few particles to carry the heat away.
