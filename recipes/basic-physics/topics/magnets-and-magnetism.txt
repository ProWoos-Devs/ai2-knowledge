Magnets and magnetism

WHAT MAGNETS ARE

A magnet is an object made from magnetic materials that produces an invisible area of magnetic force around itself called a magnetic field. Magnets can pull (attract) certain metals toward themselves or push (repel) other magnets away, without needing to touch them physically.
Never put a magnet in your mouth. Small strong magnets are very dangerous if swallowed.

MAGNETIC POLES: NORTH AND SOUTH

Every magnet, no matter its size or shape, has two kinds of magnetic pole. On a bar magnet or a horseshoe magnet there is one at each end:
- The North pole (N)
- The South pole (S)
The fundamental rule of magnetism is: Opposite poles attract, and like poles repel.
- If you bring a North pole close to a South pole, they snap together with strong attraction.
- If you bring two North poles (or two South poles) together, they push each other away with a noticeable repelling force.

WHAT MAGNETS ATTRACT

Magnets do not stick to all metals. Magnets attract only specific ferromagnetic materials:
- Magnetic metals: Iron, most kinds of steel (which contains iron), nickel, and cobalt. Paperclips, steel nails, tin cans, and steel refrigerator doors stick to magnets firmly.
- Non-magnetic metals: Aluminum (soda cans, foil), copper (electrical wires, water pipes), gold, silver, and brass are NOT attracted to magnets.
- Non-magnetic materials: Wood, plastic, rubber, glass, and paper never stick to magnets.

PLANET EARTH IS A GIANT MAGNET

Deep inside planet Earth, around a solid center, lies a churning layer of hot liquid iron and nickel. As Earth spins on its axis, this swirling metal acts like a colossal dynamo, generating a massive magnetic field that envelops the entire planet and reaches far out into space.
Earth has a magnetic North Pole and a magnetic South Pole. This planetary magnetic shield helps protect life on Earth from harmful radiation from space and from the solar wind sent out by the Sun.

HOW A COMPASS WORKS

A navigational compass is simply a lightweight, magnetized needle balanced freely on a sharp central pivot:
- The north-seeking tip of the compass needle lines up with Earth's magnetic field and points roughly north, toward the magnetic North Pole in the Arctic.
- Here is a puzzle: opposite poles attract, so the pole of Earth's magnet up there is really a south pole. People call that place the magnetic North Pole only because the north ends of compass needles point to it.
- For centuries, travelers, sailors, hikers, and explorers have relied on compasses to find their way across vast oceans, dense forests, and featureless deserts, day or night, rain or shine.
