Sound and vibrations

HOW SOUND IS MADE

Sound is a form of energy made by vibrating objects. Whenever something shakes or vibrates rapidly back and forth, it pushes against the air particles next to it. Those particles bump into their neighbors, creating invisible ripples of compression and stretching called sound waves. You can feel this vibration by gently placing your fingers on your throat while humming.

HOW SOUND TRAVELS

Sound needs a physical material (called a medium) to travel through. Sound waves pass through solids, liquids, and gases:
- In air: Sound travels at about 340 meters per second (about 1,225 km/h).
- In water: Sound travels much faster, around 1,500 meters per second, which helps whales hear each other over very long distances.
- In solid steel: Sound zooms at over 5,000 meters per second because steel is very stiff. Its atoms are held firmly to their neighbors and pass the bumps along very quickly.

NO SOUND IN OUTER SPACE

Because sound requires vibrating particles to travel, sound cannot travel through a vacuum (empty space where there are no air particles). In outer space, there is absolute silence; movie explosions in deep space would be completely silent in real life.

PITCH: HIGH AND LOW NOTES

Pitch describes how high or low a sound squeaks or rumbles:
- High pitch: Made by rapid, fast vibrations with high frequency (many waves per second). Examples: a whistle, a squeaking mouse, a small flute, or a thin violin string.
- Low pitch: Made by slow, lazy vibrations with low frequency. Examples: deep thunder, a roaring lion, a tuba, or a thick bass guitar string.
Shortening a vibrating string or column of air makes the pitch higher; lengthening it makes the pitch lower.

VOLUME: LOUD AND QUIET SOUNDS

Volume (loudness) describes the strength and energy carried by a sound wave:
- Gentle vibrations create low-energy sound waves with small height (amplitude), producing a soft whisper or ticking clock.
- Forceful vibrations create high-energy sound waves with tall amplitude, producing a loud shout, slamming door, or drum beat.
Loudness is measured in decibels (dB). Whispers are about 30 dB, normal speaking is about 60 dB, and loud rock concerts or sirens can reach 110 dB or more.
Very loud sounds can permanently damage your hearing, and the damage can happen before your ears hurt. Keep sound experiments gentle and away from ears. Turn headphones down, take listening breaks, and move away from loud speakers or machinery.

HOW ECHOES FORM

An echo is a sound that has bounced off a large, hard, flat surface (such as a sheer cliff face, a brick wall, or the walls of an empty cave) and returned back to your ears a moment later. Soft materials like curtains, carpets, and cushions absorb sound waves instead of bouncing them, preventing echoes and making rooms quiet.
