Friction and grip

Search terms: friction, grip, rough and smooth, resistance, sliding, slowing down, brakes, oil, lubrication, treads.

Written for AI-2. Directed to children, parents and teachers.

WHAT FRICTION IS

Friction is a contact force that resists motion when two surfaces slide or rub against each other. Friction always acts in the opposite direction to the movement, working to slow down sliding objects and eventually bring them to a complete stop. When you slide a wooden toy car across a carpet, friction between the wheels and the carpet fibres brings the car to a halt quickly.

ROUGH SURFACES VERSUS SMOOTH SURFACES

All surfaces, even ones that look completely flat to the naked eye, have tiny microscopic bumps, ridges, and valleys.
- Rough surfaces (like sandpaper, concrete, or carpet) have large ridges that catch on each other, producing strong friction.
- Smooth surfaces (like polished ice, glass, or wet tiles) have much smaller bumps, so they slide past each other easily with very little friction.

WHEN FRICTION IS USEFUL

Friction is essential for everyday life; without friction, we could not even stand up or walk:
- Walking and running: The friction between the soles of your shoes and the ground pushes you forward without slipping. Walking on smooth ice is difficult because there is almost no friction to grip your shoes.
- Brakes: Bicycle hand brakes squeeze rubber pads against the metal wheel rims. The high friction slows the spinning wheels and stops the bike.
- Car tires: Deep rubber treads on tires grip rainy roads and prevent the car from skidding.
- Writing: The friction between a pencil tip and paper rubs off small particles of graphite, leaving a clear mark.

WHEN FRICTION IS A PROBLEM

Friction can also cause unwanted problems by wearing things out and wasting energy as heat:
- Squeaky hinges: Rubbing metal in door hinges squeaks and grinds down the metal over time.
- Engine heat: Moving machine parts rub together, creating dangerous heat that can melt parts if not cooled.

HOW TO REDUCE FRICTION (LUBRICATION)

Friction can be reduced by making surfaces smoother or by adding a slippery liquid called a lubricant.
- Putting drops of oil or grease between metal parts coats the microscopic bumps so they slide past without catching.
- Placing round balls (ball bearings) between wheels and axles swaps sliding friction for rolling friction, which is much weaker and lets wheels spin freely.
