Food touched by floodwater

Search terms: flood, floodwater, flooded kitchen, storm water got in, flooded cupboard, can I eat food after a flood, wet food packages, flooded fridge, flooded freezer.

Written from USDA, FoodSafety.gov and CDC sources. Not reviewed by a food safety specialist.
Last reviewed: 2026-11-27

For: Food, packages and kitchen items that floodwater or storm water may have reached

Not for: Cleaning cans and pouches to save them. See Saving cans and pouches after a flood.

USE THIS TOPIC WHEN

- Floodwater or storm water has been in the kitchen, the cupboards, the basement or the refrigerator

WHAT TO DO

1. Do not eat any food that may have touched floodwater. [USDA, CDC]

2. Throw out food that is not in a waterproof container if there is any chance floodwater reached it. Containers with screw caps, snap lids, pull tops, crimped caps, twist caps and flip tops are not waterproof. [USDA, CDC]

3. Throw out food in cardboard boxes, including juice, milk and baby formula boxes, and all home-canned food that floodwater touched. They cannot be cleaned well enough. [USDA, CDC]

4. Throw out food in damaged cans. Damage means swelling, leaking, punctures, holes, cracks, deep rust, or dents bad enough that the can will not stack or open with a hand can opener. [USDA, CDC]

5. Throw out wooden cutting boards, plastic utensils, baby bottle nipples and pacifiers that floodwater may have touched. There is no way to clean them safely. [USDA, CDC]

6. Undamaged factory-made cans and retort pouches can be saved. See Saving cans and pouches after a flood. [USDA, CDC]

7. Clean metal pans, ceramic dishes, utensils and counters before use. See Cleaning dishes and counters after a flood. [USDA, CDC]

8. Use drinking water that floodwater has not touched. [USDA]

DO NOT

- Do not use a refrigerator or freezer that floodwater reached, even partly. USDA says it is unsafe and must be thrown out. [USDA]

- Do not eat perishable food that the power cut left warm. See Refrigerator food: keep or throw out. [CDC]

IF THINGS CHANGE

- If a can spurts liquid or foam when you open it, or the food is discolored, moldy or smells bad, throw it out. [CDC]

WITH NO SUPPLIES

- Without soap, bleach or clean water you cannot clean flooded containers as the sources describe. Throw out the food in them. [USDA, CDC]

ALTERNATIVES USING WHAT IS AVAILABLE

- Moving food up out of reach before the water comes saves it. See Getting ready before the power goes. [USDA]

IF HELP IS DELAYED OR CANNOT BE REACHED

- Floodwater carries what it passed through. Unsafe food can make you sick even if it looks and smells normal. [CDC]

WHAT HAS NO EFFECTIVE SUBSTITUTE

- Throwing out what cannot be cleaned: cardboard, home-canned food, wood, soft plastic, nipples and pacifiers. [USDA, CDC]

WHERE SOURCES DIFFER OR ARE SILENT

- USDA adds plastic utensils to the items to throw out; the CDC page names only wooden cutting boards, nipples and pacifiers.

- The refrigerator rule above is from USDA only.

LEFT OFF THIS TOPIC

- Making flood-touched water safe to drink. See the Water and Sanitation Knowledge Pack.

Source: USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service, Keep Your Food Safe During Emergencies, last updated 2013 (USDA); FoodSafety.gov, Food Safety During Power Outage charts, reviewed 2024 (Chart); CDC, Keep Food Safe After a Disaster or Emergency, 2025 (CDC).
