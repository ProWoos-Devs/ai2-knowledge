Cleaning a refrigerator that smells

Search terms: fridge smells, freezer smell after power cut, rotten food smell, clean the fridge, odor, mildew, baking soda, vinegar, coffee grounds, newspaper in fridge.

Written from USDA, FoodSafety.gov and CDC sources. Not reviewed by a food safety specialist.
Last reviewed: 2026-11-27

For: A refrigerator or freezer that smells after food spoiled in it

Not for: A refrigerator or freezer that floodwater reached. USDA says it must be thrown out. See Food touched by floodwater.

USE THIS TOPIC WHEN

- Food spoiled during a power cut and the refrigerator or freezer smells

WHAT TO DO

The steps may have to be repeated several times. [USDA]

1. Throw out all spoiled or doubtful food. [USDA]

2. Take out the shelves, drawers and ice trays. Wash them well with hot water and detergent, then rinse with 1 tablespoon (about 15 mL) of unscented liquid chlorine bleach per US gallon (about 3.8 liters) of drinking water. [USDA]

3. Wash the inside, including the door and its rubber seals, with hot water and baking soda (bicarbonate of soda). Rinse with the same bleach solution. [USDA]

4. Leave the door open for about 15 minutes. [USDA]

DO NOT

- Do not use scented bleach. [USDA]

IF THINGS CHANGE

- If the smell remains, try any of these: wipe the inside with equal parts vinegar and water; leave the door open to air for several days; fill it with rolled newspaper, close the door for several days, then remove the paper and clean with vinegar and water; or put fresh coffee grounds or baking soda loosely in a large shallow container on the bottom. [USDA]

- If the smell still remains, the refrigerator may need to be thrown out. [USDA]

WITH NO SUPPLIES

- Airing it with the door open for several days needs nothing. [USDA]

ALTERNATIVES USING WHAT IS AVAILABLE

- Vinegar, baking soda, coffee grounds or newspaper. [USDA]

IF HELP IS DELAYED OR CANNOT BE REACHED

- Emptying, washing and airing need no power. [USDA]

WHAT HAS NO EFFECTIVE SUBSTITUTE

- Removing the spoiled food first. [USDA]

WHERE SOURCES DIFFER OR ARE SILENT

- Only USDA covers this. The metric figures are our own conversion.

- Neither source says how strong the bleach is; both were written for United States household bleach. See the Water and Sanitation Knowledge Pack for reading the strength on a bleach label.

LEFT OFF THIS TOPIC

- Commercial odor products. USDA mentions them; follow the maker's instructions.

Source: USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service, Keep Your Food Safe During Emergencies, last updated 2013 (USDA); FoodSafety.gov, Food Safety During Power Outage charts, reviewed 2024 (Chart); CDC, Keep Food Safe After a Disaster or Emergency, 2025 (CDC).
