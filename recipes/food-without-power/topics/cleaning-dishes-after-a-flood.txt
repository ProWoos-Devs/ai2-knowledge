Cleaning dishes and counters after a flood

Search terms: clean pots after flood, sanitize dishes, bleach solution for kitchen, wash counters, countertop, utensils, can opener, kitchen after flood, disinfect plates.

Written from USDA, FoodSafety.gov and CDC sources. Not reviewed by a food safety specialist.
Last reviewed: 2026-11-27

For: Metal pans, ceramic dishes, utensils, refrigerator drawers and counters that floodwater touched

Not for: Wooden cutting boards, plastic utensils, baby bottle nipples and pacifiers. Those are thrown out. See Food touched by floodwater.

USE THIS TOPIC WHEN

- Floodwater has reached dishes, pans, utensils or kitchen surfaces

WHAT TO DO

1. Wash them with soap and water, hot if you have it. Include the can opener. [USDA, CDC]

2. Rinse with clean, safe water. [CDC, USDA]

3. Sanitize them in one of these ways:
   - Boil dishes, pans and utensils in clean water. [USDA]
   - Or soak them in a bleach solution. USDA: 15 minutes in 1 tablespoon (about 15 mL) of unscented liquid chlorine bleach per US gallon (about 3.8 liters) of drinking water. CDC: 1 minute in 1 cup (240 mL) of unscented household chlorine bleach in 5 US gallons (about 19 liters) of clean water, with the items fully covered. [USDA, CDC]

4. For counters and anything that cannot go into a bucket, wipe the bleach solution on with a cloth. [CDC, USDA]

5. Let everything air-dry. [CDC, USDA]

DO NOT

- Do not try to clean wooden cutting boards, plastic utensils, baby bottle nipples or pacifiers that floodwater touched. Throw them out. [USDA, CDC]

- Do not use scented bleach. [USDA, CDC]

IF THINGS CHANGE

- For cookware exposed to fire-fighting chemicals, see Food near a fire. [USDA]

WITH NO SUPPLIES

- Without bleach, USDA gives boiling in clean water for dishes, pans and utensils. Neither source gives a way to sanitize counters without bleach. [USDA]

ALTERNATIVES USING WHAT IS AVAILABLE

- USDA says hot water if it is available, so washing with water that is not hot is still the first step. [USDA]

IF HELP IS DELAYED OR CANNOT BE REACHED

- When bleach runs out, dishes, pans and utensils can still be sanitized by boiling them in clean water. [USDA]

WHAT HAS NO EFFECTIVE SUBSTITUTE

- Washing before sanitizing. Dirt left on items makes chlorine work less well. [USDA]

WHERE SOURCES DIFFER OR ARE SILENT

- USDA gives no boiling time for dishes, pans and utensils. For cans and pouches both sources say 2 minutes at a boil.

- The two bleach solutions differ, and so do the soaking times: the CDC solution is about three times as strong and soaks for 1 minute, the USDA one soaks for 15 minutes. Both are given as each source states them. The metric figures are partly our own conversion.

- Neither source says how strong the bleach is; both were written for United States household bleach. See the Water and Sanitation Knowledge Pack for reading the strength on a bleach label.

LEFT OFF THIS TOPIC

- Cleaning the rest of a flooded house. Not in these sources.

Source: USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service, Keep Your Food Safe During Emergencies, last updated 2013 (USDA); FoodSafety.gov, Food Safety During Power Outage charts, reviewed 2024 (Chart); CDC, Keep Food Safe After a Disaster or Emergency, 2025 (CDC).
