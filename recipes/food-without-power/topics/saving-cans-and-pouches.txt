Saving cans and pouches after a flood

Search terms: clean cans after flood, can I keep canned food, wash tins, retort pouch, sanitize cans, bleach cans, boil cans, relabel cans, tinned food flooded.

Written from USDA, FoodSafety.gov and CDC sources. Not reviewed by a food safety specialist.
Last reviewed: 2026-11-27

For: Undamaged factory-made food in all-metal cans or retort pouches (flexible shelf-stable pouches) that floodwater touched

Not for: Damaged cans, home-canned food, cardboard boxes or screw-top jars. Those are thrown out. See Food touched by floodwater.

USE THIS TOPIC WHEN

- Floodwater reached cans or pouches that are not damaged

WHAT TO DO

1. Take off the labels if you can, and note the expiration date. Labels can hold dirt and bacteria. [USDA, CDC]

2. Brush or wipe away dirt and silt. [USDA, CDC]

3. Wash the cans or pouches with soap and water, hot if you have it. [USDA, CDC]

4. Rinse them with water that is safe to drink, if you have it. Dirt or soap left on them makes chlorine work less well. [USDA, CDC]

5. Then sanitize them in one of two ways:
   - Put them in a pot of water, bring it to a boil and keep boiling for 2 minutes. [CDC, USDA]
   - Or soak them for 15 minutes in a freshly made bleach solution. USDA: 1 tablespoon (about 15 mL) of unscented liquid chlorine bleach per US gallon (about 3.8 liters) of drinking water, or the cleanest, clearest water you have. CDC: 1 cup (240 mL) of unscented household bleach in 5 US gallons (about 19 liters) of water. [USDA, CDC]

6. Let them air-dry for at least 1 hour before opening or storing. [USDA]

7. Write on each can or pouch with a marker what is inside and the expiration date. [USDA, CDC]

8. Use the food as soon as possible. [USDA, CDC]

DO NOT

- Do not try to save damaged, bulging or leaking cans. [USDA, CDC]

- Do not use scented bleach. Both sources say unscented. [USDA, CDC]

IF THINGS CHANGE

- Concentrated baby formula in a cleaned all-metal can must be mixed with clean drinking water. [USDA]

WITH NO SUPPLIES

- With no bleach, boiling for 2 minutes is the other method both sources give. [USDA, CDC]

ALTERNATIVES USING WHAT IS AVAILABLE

- USDA allows the cleanest, clearest water available for the bleach solution if there is no drinking water. [USDA]

IF HELP IS DELAYED OR CANNOT BE REACHED

- Both sources say to use cleaned cans and pouches as soon as possible, so do not set them aside as long-term stores. [USDA, CDC]

WHAT HAS NO EFFECTIVE SUBSTITUTE

- An undamaged can. Cleaning does not make a damaged one safe. [USDA, CDC]

WHERE SOURCES DIFFER OR ARE SILENT

- The two bleach solutions differ: the CDC one is about three times as strong as the USDA one. Both are given as each source states them. The metric figures for the USDA solution and the liters for the CDC one are our own conversion.

- Neither source says how strong the bleach is; both were written for United States household bleach. See the Water and Sanitation Knowledge Pack for reading the strength on a bleach label.

- CDC also includes food in plastic pouches; USDA names all-metal cans and retort pouches.

LEFT OFF THIS TOPIC

- Glass jars. Screw-top and crimped lids are not waterproof, so the food is thrown out. [USDA]

Source: USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service, Keep Your Food Safe During Emergencies, last updated 2013 (USDA); FoodSafety.gov, Food Safety During Power Outage charts, reviewed 2024 (Chart); CDC, Keep Food Safe After a Disaster or Emergency, 2025 (CDC).
