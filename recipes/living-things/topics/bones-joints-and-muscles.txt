SUPPORT AND PROTECTION

The skeleton is the framework of bones inside the body. It supports soft body parts and works with muscles to produce movement. Bones in the legs support the body when standing. The skull surrounds the brain; the backbone protects the spinal cord, the column of nerve tissue connected to the brain; the rib cage helps protect the heart and lungs. These structures provide protection while also being parts of a living body. [S8, Introduction to the Skeletal System; S10A, Spinal Cord; web.]

Bones are living structures. They contain tissues, groups of cells working together. They use oxygen and nutrients, useful substances from food, and need a blood supply. Bone can change its structure in response to forces placed on it. A skeleton is more than a fixed model of dry white pieces. [S8, living-bone explanation; S36, Vocabulary, Tissue; S13, Why is digestion important?; web.]

WHERE BONES MEET

A joint is a place where bones meet. Some joints allow a great deal of movement, some only a little, and some are immovable. The joined bones of the skull provide examples of immovable joints. Most joints in the adult body are freely movable. Your elbow and hip are examples: the elbow bends and straightens, while the hip allows movement in several directions. Structure affects the movement a joint permits. [S8A, Articulations, Synarthroses, Amphiarthroses and Diarthroses; S44, What are joints? and How do joints move?; web.]

In freely movable joints, a tissue called cartilage covers the ends of the bones. A capsule encloses the joint, ligaments help hold the bones together, and fluid inside lubricates their movement. These soft tissues matter as well as the bones themselves. A skeleton drawing that shows only bone leaves out parts needed to understand a working joint. [S8A, Diarthroses; web.]

HOW MUSCLES MOVE BONES

Skeletal muscles contain bundles of specialized muscle cells. Contraction means tightening and producing a pulling force. A contracting muscle can shorten and pull on the bones attached by tendons. Nerve signals start the contraction, and blood vessels supply the muscle. [S9, Introduction to the Muscular System; S9A, Structure of Skeletal Muscle; S36, How do muscles work? and Vocabulary; web.]

Muscles can work in opposing pairs. When the biceps contracts to bend an arm, the triceps relaxes; to straighten it, the triceps contracts and the biceps relaxes. Contracting muscles can also exert force while being lengthened, so contraction does not always mean getting shorter. [S37, Muscle, opposing-pair and eccentric-contraction explanations; web.]

Muscles also help maintain posture, meaning how the body is held. They adjust when a person sits or stands and help stabilize joints. Muscles can work even when the body appears still. [S9, posture and joint stability; web.]

MORE THAN THE MUSCLES YOU NOTICE

Skeletal muscle helps move the skeleton. Other muscle types include cardiac muscle in the heart and smooth muscle in organs such as the digestive tract. Smooth and cardiac muscle are involuntary: you cannot control their contractions just by deciding to move them. Bones have other jobs too: red marrow within certain bones produces blood cells. The body's support, movement and internal work are closely connected. [S9B, Muscle Types; S8, marrow explanation; web.]
