A LIFE HAS STAGES

A life cycle describes stages in an organism's life and how its kind produces another generation. A young animal may resemble its parents, or it may look very different. Growth means becoming larger; development also includes changes in body form and what an animal can do. Looking at only one stage can hide much of the story. [S1, Background; S5, Life Cycle; S16, hatching discussion; web.]

Butterflies provide a clear example of large changes in form. A monarch butterfly goes through egg, caterpillar, pupa and adult stages. These are stages of the same kind of animal, not four unrelated creatures. The large change from young form to adult form is called metamorphosis. The word describes development, not a change into a different species. [S5, Life Cycle; web.]

EGG AND CATERPILLAR

A female monarch lays eggs on milkweed plants. A caterpillar hatches from an egg and feeds on milkweed leaves. Caterpillar is the familiar name for this larval stage. The larva's feeding supports its growth. It sheds its outer skin as it grows, rather than keeping one unchanged covering throughout its young life. [S5, Behavior and Diet and Life Cycle; web.]

The monarch's young and adult stages use different foods. The caterpillar eats leaves, while the adult sips nectar from flowers through a tube-like mouthpart. This is a useful reminder that an animal's needs can change during its life. "What does a butterfly eat?" may need an answer about which stage is meant. [S5, Behavior and Diet; web.]

PUPA AND ADULT

After its caterpillar stages, the monarch enters the pupal stage and forms a chrysalis. Development toward the adult continues during this stage. The still-looking outside does not mean nothing is happening. An adult butterfly eventually emerges. Adults can reproduce, beginning another generation with eggs. [S5, Life Cycle; web.]

A stage diagram uses arrows to show a sequence. Egg -> caterpillar -> pupa -> adult describes the monarch. An arrow from adult back to egg means adults produce the next generation; it does not mean one old butterfly turns back into its own egg. The stages take time, and their timing depends on conditions. [S5, Life Cycle; web.]

OTHER ANIMALS HAVE OTHER PATTERNS

A frog's young stage can be a tadpole that develops legs and loses its tail as it becomes an adult frog. A newly hatched alligator already looks much more like a small adult alligator. Neither example follows the monarch's egg-caterpillar-pupa-adult sequence. Life-cycle diagrams should name the animal they describe, rather than presenting one animal's stages as a rule for every animal.
