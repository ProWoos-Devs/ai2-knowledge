MORE THAN MAKING FOOD SMALLER

Digestion breaks food into parts that the body can absorb and use. Food provides nutrients, including carbohydrates, fats and proteins, as well as vitamins, minerals and water. The body uses nutrients for energy, growth and repair. Digestion includes both physical movements, such as chewing and mixing, and chemical changes made possible by digestive juices. [S13, Why is digestion important? and How does my digestive system break food into small parts my body can use?; web.]

The digestive tract is a connected passage from the mouth to the anus, the opening where stool leaves the body. It includes the esophagus, stomach, small intestine and large intestine. Other organs, including the liver and pancreas, help digestion by supplying substances through small tubes. Food itself does not travel through every organ that helps digest it. [S13, What is the digestive system? and digestive-juice sections; web.]

MOUTH, FOOD PIPE AND STOMACH

Chewing breaks food into smaller pieces. Saliva moistens it and begins breaking down starch, one kind of carbohydrate. After swallowing, food travels along the esophagus, the food pipe, toward the stomach. Waves of muscle action move it onward. This movement is called peristalsis and also occurs farther along the digestive tract. [S13, How does food move through my GI tract? and Mouth; web.]

The stomach mixes food with digestive juices, including acid and enzymes. Enzymes are substances that help break food down chemically. The stomach slowly passes its contents into the small intestine. Digestion does not finish in the stomach, and the stomach is not where most nutrients enter the body. [S13, Stomach and What happens to the digested food?; web.]

SMALL INTESTINE AND HELPING ORGANS

In the small intestine, digestive juices continue breaking food down. The pancreas supplies juice containing enzymes, and the liver makes bile, which helps digest fats. The gallbladder stores bile between meals. These organs cooperate with the intestine rather than each digesting a separate meal on its own. [S13, Pancreas, Liver, Gallbladder and Small intestine; web.]

Most nutrients are absorbed through the small intestine. Absorption means moving digested substances across its lining so they can be carried elsewhere. Blood and lymph, another body transport system, carry absorbed nutrients. Digestion and absorption are related jobs, but they are not the same process. [S13, What happens to the digested food?; web.]

WHAT REMAINS

The large intestine absorbs water from the remaining material. Gut bacteria also help break down some substances. Waste becomes stool, or poop, which is stored in the rectum, the last part of the large intestine, before leaving through the anus. Stool includes undigested material and older cells; it is not simply a pile containing every bit of food a person ate. [S13, Large intestine, Rectum and How does my digestive system work?; web.]
