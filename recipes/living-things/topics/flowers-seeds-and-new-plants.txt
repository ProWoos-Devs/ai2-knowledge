FLOWERS AND POLLEN

Flowers take part in the reproduction of flowering plants. Pollen consists of tiny grains produced in flower parts called anthers. Pollination happens when pollen reaches a receptive flower part called the stigma of the same kind of plant. Pollen can travel to a flower on the same plant or another plant of the same kind. Pollination is a step toward forming seeds, not the same event as a seed sprouting. [S3, PDF pp. 3–4, What is Pollination? and A Good Pollinator.]

After suitable pollen reaches a stigma, a pollen tube can grow toward the ovary, a part inside the flower. It delivers sperm cells. Fertilization occurs when a sperm cell joins an egg cell, starting the young plant inside a seed. Many pollinated flowers develop fruits containing seeds. Certain fruits and seeds are not produced without pollination. A seedling is a young plant after sprouting. [S3, PDF p. 3; S38, PDF pp. 10–11, Fertilization and Fruit and Seed Development; S27, Germination; web where unpaginated.]

POLLINATORS AND OTHER JOURNEYS

Many flowers produce nectar, food that attracts visiting animals. Pollen sticks to a visitor and can rub off on another flower. Animals carrying pollen this way, including bees, are called pollinators. Some plants instead depend on wind or water to carry pollen. Not every flowering plant needs a bee, and not every pollinator is an insect: some birds and bats move pollen too. [S3, PDF pp. 3–4, What is Pollination? and Meet the Pollinators; S5, Behavior and Diet; S51, PDF p. 3, Why do pollinators visit flowers? and Pollinator Syndrome Traits Table; web where unpaginated.]

Seeds can also travel. Wind carries light seeds with fluffy or winglike structures. Some seeds float; others catch on animals. Some survive being eaten with fruit and are carried elsewhere before leaving the animal's body. These are methods of seed dispersal, spreading seeds away from the parent. Moving pollen and moving seeds are different journeys. [S2, Background, seed dispersal; web.]

HOW A SEED SPROUTS

Germination is the beginning of a seed's growth into a seedling. Water enters the seed, internal processes make stored food usable, and cells begin dividing and growing. A developing root and shoot emerge. In a typical land-plant seedling, the root grows downward and the shoot grows upward. The seed's food reserves support the young plant during these early stages. [S29, PDF p. 1, Seed to Soil Contact; S27, Germination; web where unpaginated.]

Seeds need water, suitable temperatures and oxygen for germination. Light requirements vary, and some seeds remain dormant, delaying growth. Different kinds do not all sprout under identical conditions. Early growth uses food stored in the seed; later, green parts use light to make food by photosynthesis. [S38, PDF pp. 21–23, Variation in Dormancy and Environmental Factors; S27, Germination; S26, photosynthesis explanation; web where unpaginated.]

Flowers and seeds are not the only route to new plants. A broken willow branch can take root in wet soil. Ferns do not make flowers or seeds; they produce tiny spores instead. These examples show why the flowering-plant sequence is not a rule for every plant. [S2, Willow; S46, opening fern description; web.]
