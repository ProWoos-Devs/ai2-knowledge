FLOWERS AND POLLEN

Flowers take part in the reproduction of flowering plants. Pollen is produced in flower parts called anthers. Pollination happens when pollen reaches a receptive flower part called the stigma of the same kind of plant. Pollen can travel between flowers or between parts of one flower. Pollination is a step toward forming seeds, not the same event as a seed sprouting. [S3, PDF p. 3, What is Pollination?]

After suitable pollen reaches a stigma, a pollen tube can grow toward the ovary, a part inside the flower. Fertilization can then occur and seeds develop. These are separate stages, so seeing pollen on a flower does not mean a new seedling already exists. A seedling is a young developing plant. [S3, PDF p. 3; S27, Germination; web where unpaginated.]

POLLINATORS AND OTHER JOURNEYS

Bees and other animals can move pollen while visiting flowers. Pollen sticks to a visitor and can rub off on another flower. These animals are called pollinators. Some plants instead depend on wind or water to carry pollen. Not every flowering plant needs a bee, and not every pollinator is an insect: some birds and bats move pollen too. [S3, PDF pp. 3–4, What is Pollination? and Meet the Pollinators.]

Seeds can also travel. Wind carries light seeds with fluffy or winglike structures. Some seeds float; others catch on animals. Some survive being eaten with fruit and are carried elsewhere before leaving the animal's body. These are methods of seed dispersal, spreading seeds away from the parent. Moving pollen and moving seeds are different journeys. [S2, Background, seed dispersal; web.]

HOW A SEED SPROUTS

Germination is the beginning of a seed's growth into a seedling. Water enters the seed, internal processes make stored food usable, and cells begin dividing and growing. A developing root and shoot emerge. In a typical land-plant seedling, the root grows downward and the shoot grows upward. The seed's food reserves support the young plant during these early stages. [S29, PDF p. 1, Seed to Soil Contact; S27, Germination; web where unpaginated.]

Suitable moisture and temperature matter for germination. Seeds are not all ready to sprout at the same moment or under identical conditions. The young plant's early growth uses reserves already in the seed; it does not begin by collecting a full meal from the soil. Later, green parts use light to make food by photosynthesis. [S29, PDF p. 1; S27, Germination; S26, photosynthesis explanation; web where unpaginated.]
