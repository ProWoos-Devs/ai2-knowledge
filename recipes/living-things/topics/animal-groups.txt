START WITH A BACKBONE

Animals can be compared by features of their bodies. Vertebrates have a backbone, also called a vertebral column. Humans belong to this group. Invertebrates do not have a backbone. An invertebrate is still an animal: butterflies and spiders are examples. "Animal" does not mean only a large furry creature, and having a hard outer covering is different from having a backbone. [S4, Introduction; S14, opening requirements; S19, Insects and Arachnids; web.]

MAMMALS AND BIRDS

Mammals have hair or fur, breathe air with lungs, and belong to species whose mothers produce milk for their young. People, bats and dolphins are mammals. Living in water does not automatically make an animal a fish, and flying does not automatically make it a bird. A dolphin breathes air even though it lives in water. [S14, opening requirements and examples; web.]

Birds have feathers and wings. Many fly, but penguins, emus and ostriches do not. Wings alone therefore do not establish whether an animal can fly. The features used to recognize a group are different from an activity that only some of its members can do. Compare a bat with a bird: both can have wings, but their body coverings and other features differ. [S35, All About Birds and Physical Characteristics; S15, opening flight discussion; S14, examples; web.]

REPTILES, AMPHIBIANS AND FISH

Reptiles such as lizards, snakes, turtles and crocodiles have scaly body coverings. Amphibians such as frogs and salamanders have a different kind of skin, generally moist rather than covered in the thick dry scales of those reptiles. Amphibian skin can take part in gas exchange. Many amphibians have young that develop in water. These familiar groups contain a great variety of animals. [S16, examples; S17, skin and life-cycle discussion; web.]

Fish have gills throughout their lives and fin-shaped limbs rather than hands with fingers. Gills are breathing structures that take oxygen from water. A fish and a dolphin can both swim, but their breathing structures and other body features place them in different groups. Where an animal lives is one useful clue, not the whole answer. [S18, opening physical features; S33, So, how do gills work?; S14, opening requirements; web.]

INSECTS AND SPIDERS

A typical adult insect has six legs, two antennae and three main body regions: head, thorax and abdomen. The thorax is the middle region. Insects have an exoskeleton, a firm outer covering. Spiders have eight legs and two main body regions, and are arachnids rather than insects. Both are invertebrates. A caterpillar is an insect's young stage, so use the adult stage when comparing the familiar six-legged body plan. [S19, Insects and Arachnids; S5, Life Cycle; web.]
