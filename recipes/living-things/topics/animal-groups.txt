START WITH A BACKBONE

Animals can be compared by features of their bodies. Vertebrates have a backbone, also called a vertebral column. Humans belong to this group. Invertebrates do not have a backbone. An invertebrate is still an animal: butterflies, spiders, centipedes and millipedes are examples. Crustaceans are invertebrates too; krill are small, shrimp-like crustaceans. "Animal" does not mean only a large furry creature, and having a hard outer covering is different from having a backbone. MAMMALS AND BIRDS

Mammals have hair or fur, breathe air with lungs, and belong to species whose mothers produce milk for their young. People, mice and bats are mammals. A dolphin does not look hairy, yet it breathes air and feeds its young on milk. Living in water does not automatically make an animal a fish; flying does not automatically make it a bird. [S14, opening requirements and examples; web.]

Birds have feathers and wings. Many fly, but penguins, emus and ostriches do not. Wings alone therefore do not establish whether an animal can fly. The features used to recognize a group are different from an activity that only some of its members can do. Compare a bat with a bird: both can have wings, but their body coverings and other features differ. [S35, All About Birds and Physical Characteristics; S15, opening flight discussion; S14, examples; web.]

REPTILES, AMPHIBIANS AND FISH

Reptiles such as lizards, snakes, turtles and crocodiles have scaly body coverings. Amphibians such as frogs and salamanders have a different kind of skin, generally moist rather than covered in the thick dry scales of those reptiles. Many amphibians can take in oxygen through their skin, and some also breathe with lungs. Many amphibians have young that develop in water. These familiar groups contain a great variety of animals. [S16, examples; S17, skin and life-cycle discussion; web.]

Fish have gills throughout their lives and fin-shaped limbs rather than hands with fingers. Gills are breathing structures that take oxygen from water. Fish can have scales too, so scales alone do not mean an animal is a reptile. A fish and a dolphin can both swim, but their breathing structures and other body features place them in different groups. Where an animal lives is one useful clue, not the whole answer. [S18, opening physical features; S33, So, how do gills work?; S42, Scales and Other Structures; S14, opening requirements; web.]

INSECTS AND SPIDERS

A typical adult insect has six legs, two antennae and three main body regions: head, thorax and abdomen. The thorax is the middle region. Insects have an exoskeleton, a firm outer covering. Spiders have eight legs and two main body regions, and are arachnids rather than insects. Both are invertebrates. Butterfly young are caterpillars. Young insects can look very different from adults, so compare the adult stage when using the six-legged body plan. [S19, Insects and Arachnids; S5, Life Cycle; web.]
