Roots, stems and leaves

Written for AI-2. Directed to children, parents and teachers.

PARTS WORK TOGETHER

A familiar flowering plant has roots, a stem and leaves, as well as flowers at some stages of its life. These structures have different jobs. To understand a plant, ask what each part helps it do: obtain water, support other parts, make food or reproduce. Plants can have very different-looking parts while carrying out similar jobs. [S2, Plant Structures and Functions; S3, PDF p. 3, What is Pollination?]

Roots help anchor a tree and take up water and minerals from the soil. Desert plants show different ways roots can reach water. Some have wide, shallow roots that can collect it after rain. Others have deep roots that reach water farther below the surface. These are different structures suited to different circumstances, rather than one root shape being best everywhere. [S34, Roots; S2, Background; web.]

STEMS SUPPORT OTHER PARTS

A stem holds up leaves, buds and flowers. Stems vary greatly in shape: a cactus pad is a stem too, even though it looks unlike a tree trunk or a thin flower stalk. Looking at its job and connections is more useful than deciding that every flat green part must be a leaf. [S32, Plant Stems; S2, Background and Other Specific Desert Plant Adaptations, Cacti; web.]

A tree trunk also provides routes for water and minerals to travel from roots toward leaves and branches. Materials made in the leaves move to other parts of the tree too. Support and transport are different jobs carried out through the same structure. [S34, Trunks; web.]

HOW GREEN PARTS MAKE FOOD

Photosynthesis is the process of using light energy to make food. In this process, energy from sunlight helps combine water and carbon dioxide into sugars, releasing oxygen. Carbon dioxide and oxygen are gases. Carbon dioxide enters from the air; water comes up from the roots. Photosynthesis takes place in green material called chlorophyll. Green plants need sunlight, carbon dioxide and water to make food this way. Sugar is food; sunlight supplies energy. [S26, photosynthesis explanation; S2, Cacti; S34, Roots, Leaves and Trunks; S30, printed p. 11, Photosynthesis; web where unpaginated.]

Leaves are important sites of photosynthesis, but green stems can do it too. In cacti, the green stem carries out this work, while the spines are modified leaves. This is why "all plants make food only in their leaves" would be an unreliable rule. Different plant structures can share a job. [S2, Background and Other Specific Desert Plant Adaptations, Cacti; web.]

SHAPES HELP PLANTS LIVE

Many desert plants have small leaves or waxy coverings that reduce water loss. Shallow spreading roots and deep roots provide different ways of reaching water. A plant's features make more sense when you also consider its surroundings: rain, soil moisture, sunlight and the animals that may eat it. [S2, Background and Cacti; web.]

For a paper model, draw a flowering plant and label roots, stem, leaves and flower. Beside each part, write one job described here. Then draw a cactus and label its stem and modified leaves. Comparing these drawings shows why plant parts cannot always be identified from shape alone. The drawings are models, not a rule that every plant looks like either example. [S2, Plant Structures and Functions and Cacti; web.]
