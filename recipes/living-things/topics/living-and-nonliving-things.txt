LOOK FOR SEVERAL CLUES

An organism is a living thing. A tree, a butterfly and a person are organisms. Living things use energy, grow and carry out processes that keep them alive. Their kinds continue through reproduction, producing new living things. This does not mean every individual must produce young to count as alive. One clue on its own is not a reliable test: movement, size or appearance can mislead us. [S1, Background; S5, Life Cycle; S7, Cell Structure & Function; web.]

Plants are living things even though they do not walk from place to place. Their roots, stems and leaves have jobs that help them survive and grow. A butterfly changes greatly as it develops, while a growing tree keeps much the same overall shape. Looking different does not mean two stages cannot belong to the same kind of organism. [S2, Plant Structures and Functions; S5, Life Cycle; web.]

MOVEMENT IS NOT ENOUGH

A rock rolling downhill or water flowing through a stream is moving. That movement does not make the rock or water alive. Rocks and water are nonliving parts of an environment. Living things depend on nonliving surroundings, including water and suitable conditions. A habitat includes both its organisms and those surroundings. [S30, printed p. 1, Introduction and Background.]

Something can also have been part of a living thing without being alive now. A dead branch once belonged to a living tree. A tree can even have a dead branch while the rest remains alive. This is different from a rock, which was never an organism. When sorting examples, distinguish alive now, formerly living material, and things that were never alive. [S2, Other Specific Desert Plant Adaptations, Juniper; S30, printed p. 1.]

CELLS AND LIFE CYCLES

All organisms consist of cells, the small units that carry out life's activities. Some organisms have only one cell; others have many. Almost all human cells are too small to see individually without magnification. Being unable to see cells with your eyes does not mean an object has none. A whole tree or animal is much larger than its individual cells. [S7, Cell Structure & Function; web.]

Consider a seed. A seed that can germinate contains a young plant that can begin growing when conditions allow. Looking still is different from being dead. A seedling is the developing plant after sprouting begins. Different stages may need different observations to recognize them. A useful question is not simply "Does it move?" but "What evidence shows that it is carrying out, or can resume, the processes of life?" [S27, Germination; S29, PDF p. 1, Seed to Soil Contact; S1, Background; web where unpaginated.]
