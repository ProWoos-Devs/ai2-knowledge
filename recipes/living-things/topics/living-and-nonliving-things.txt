LOOK FOR SEVERAL CLUES

An organism is a living thing. A tree, a butterfly and a person are organisms. Fungi and tiny bacteria are living things too. Living things use energy, grow and carry out processes that keep them alive. They take in food or make it, and need water. Reproduction produces new living things, allowing their kinds to continue. One clue on its own is not a reliable test: movement, size or appearance can mislead us. [S30, printed pp. 1 and 11, Background and Glossary; S1, Background; S5, Life Cycle; S7, Cell Structure & Function; web where unpaginated.]

Plants are living things even though they do not walk from place to place. Their roots, stems and leaves have jobs that help them survive and grow. A butterfly changes greatly as it develops. A plant changes too: a seed looks very different from a grown plant. Looking different does not mean two stages cannot belong to the same kind of organism. [S2, Plant Structures and Functions; S1, Background; S5, Life Cycle; S27, Germination; web.]

MOVEMENT IS NOT ENOUGH

A rock rolling downhill or water flowing through a stream is moving. That movement does not make the rock or water alive. Rocks and water are nonliving parts of an environment. Living things depend on nonliving surroundings, including water and suitable conditions. A habitat includes both its organisms and those surroundings. [S30, printed p. 1, Introduction and Background.]

Something can have been part of a living thing without being alive now. A dead branch once belonged to a living tree. A juniper can cut off water to a branch during a drought, leaving it dead while the rest of the tree survives. The dead branch is not alive now, but its history includes being part of a living thing. Calling rocks and water nonliving does not mean they died. [S2, Other Specific Desert Plant Adaptations, Juniper; S30, printed p. 1.]

CELLS AND LIFE CYCLES

All organisms consist of cells, the small units that carry out life's activities. Some organisms have only one cell; others have many. Almost all human cells are too small to see individually without magnification. Being unable to see individual cells with your eyes does not mean a living thing has none. A whole tree or animal is much larger than its individual cells. [S7, Cell Structure & Function; web.]

Consider a seed. A seed that can germinate contains a young plant that can begin growing when conditions allow. Looking still is different from being dead. A seedling is the developing plant after sprouting begins. Different stages may need different observations to recognize them. A useful question is not simply "Does it move?" but "What evidence shows that it is carrying out, or can resume, the processes of life?"
