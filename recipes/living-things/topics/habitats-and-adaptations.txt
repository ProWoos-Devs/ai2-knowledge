A HABITAT MEETS NEEDS

A habitat is the place where an organism lives, together with its living and nonliving surroundings. For an animal, consider food, water, shelter and space, and whether these are arranged so the animal can use them. A place may contain water but still be unsuitable if food or shelter is too far away. A habitat is more than a nest or sleeping place. [S30, printed p. 1, Background and Arrangement.]

Living surroundings include other organisms. Nonliving surroundings include water, rocks and weather. These parts affect each other. A change in the amount of water can change which plants grow, which can affect the animals using those plants. Describing a habitat means looking at connections, rather than making separate lists that never meet. [S30, printed pp. 1 and 5, Background and Everything Is Connected.]

FEATURES THAT HELP

An adaptation is a feature or pattern of behavior that helps an organism survive in particular surroundings. Body features can be inherited, passed from parents to young. Behavior can involve inherited patterns and learning. Food, temperature and other environmental conditions also affect organisms. A helpful feature in one setting is not necessarily helpful everywhere. [S31, Background; web.]

Camouflage is coloring or a pattern that helps an animal blend with its surroundings. Migration is movement between places and can help animals use suitable conditions at different times. These examples describe different kinds of adaptation: one concerns appearance, another behavior. Organisms have several features working together rather than one universal answer to every difficulty. [S31, Background and Vocabulary; S5, Distribution and Habitat; web.]

DESERT PLANTS

Desert plants show several ways to obtain and conserve water. Some have shallow, spreading roots that collect rain near the surface; others have deep roots. Small leaves and waxy coverings can reduce water loss. A cactus has modified leaves called spines and a green stem that carries out photosynthesis, using light to make food. [S2, Background and Cacti; web.]

These examples do not mean a plant chooses a new body part when it becomes thirsty. They describe the features of plants living in those surroundings. Compare which part takes up water and which part reduces its loss. Different structures can contribute to the same overall result: helping a plant survive where water is scarce. [S2, Background; S31, Background; web.]

ONE ANIMAL, CHANGING NEEDS

A monarch caterpillar needs milkweed leaves for food. The adult drinks nectar from flowers, and migrating monarchs use different places at different stages of their journey. Protecting only one small place may not meet an animal's needs throughout its life. On paper, connect an organism to its food, water, shelter and space; then ask what would change if one connection disappeared. [S5, Distribution and Habitat, Behavior and Diet and Life Cycle; S30, printed pp. 1 and 5.]
